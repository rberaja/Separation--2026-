## 3. Preliminary federal tax framework

### 3.1 The key point: a distribution is not automatically tax-free

The starting rule for a partnership distribution is generally favorable: a partnership ordinarily does not recognize gain or loss on a distribution, and a partner ordinarily recognizes gain only when money distributed exceeds that partner’s adjusted basis in the partnership interest. However, that is not a blanket conclusion that the planned separation is tax-free. The result depends on the full transaction, including cash, debt shifts, property basis, partner basis, historic contributions, and the sequence of steps. [IRC §731](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A731+edition%3Aprelim%29); [IRS Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541).

The fact that the owners hold their interests through limited partnerships rather than S corporations or C corporations is important, but it does not eliminate the partnership-specific exceptions below.

### 3.2 Inside basis and outside basis

- **Inside basis** means the partnership’s tax basis in each asset it owns. For this project, it includes the adjusted tax basis of each property or of the membership interest in the LLC that holds the property, including the depreciation history reflected in the tax records.
- **Outside basis** means each partner’s adjusted tax basis in that partner’s partnership interest. It is separate for every partner and is affected by contributions, allocated income and loss, distributions, and that partner’s share of partnership liabilities.

The existing property-basis information addresses inside basis. It does not, by itself, establish each owner’s outside basis. Outside-basis schedules are essential because cash and deemed cash from debt relief can produce gain if they exceed the recipient’s outside basis. A decrease in a partner’s share of partnership liabilities is generally treated as a distribution of money. [IRS Publication 541, “Effect of Partnership Liabilities”](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541).

### 3.4 Disguised-sale rules: practical safeguards

The disguised-sale rules require special attention because the project combines asset allocation, ownership equalization, and a possible cash settlement. In broad terms, a partner’s contribution of money or property followed by the partnership’s distribution of other property can be recharacterized as a sale if the distribution would not have occurred but for the contribution and the right to receive the distribution does not depend on partnership operations. Transfers occurring within two years are presumed to be a sale unless the facts clearly establish otherwise; transfers more than two years apart are presumed not to be a sale unless the facts establish otherwise. The two-year rule is an evidentiary presumption, not a safe harbor. [IRS Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541); [Treas. Reg. §1.707-3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/section-1.707-3).

The goal is not to manufacture form. The documents and actual conduct must accurately reflect a genuine separation and distribution plan. The following safeguards should be built into the transaction process:

1. **Choose and document the legal pathway before funds move.** Counsel should identify whether the transaction is an in-kind distribution, a partnership division, a liquidation, an interest transfer, or a combination. Do not label a payment a “distribution” while side agreements require it to be paid in exchange for a specified property or interest.
2. **Separate true equalization from a purchase price.** The allocation memorandum should show the valuation method, the agreed 40/60 economics, property values, debt, available cash, and how each equalization payment follows from that agreed allocation. Avoid property-by-property language that says one side is “buying” a particular asset unless counsel intentionally chooses sale treatment.
3. **Use one integrated, fully disclosed closing plan.** Maintain a dated step chart showing every contribution, distribution, debt modification, guarantor change, transfer of LLC interests, and cash movement. The plan should be reviewed before signing for direct or indirect transfers within the applicable two-year period.
4. **Do not create circular cash flows.** A partner should not contribute cash to the partnership and receive a prearranged distribution of different property or cash as a return of that contribution without tax-counsel review. The same caution applies when a new entity receives property and then pays a partner under a prearranged obligation.
5. **Treat debt changes as tax items, not merely lender items.** Although the loans are reported as non-recourse, the CPA must calculate the partners’ federal tax shares of those liabilities before and after the transaction. A lender-approved change in a bad-boy guarantor is commercially important but does not itself establish the federal income-tax allocation of the liability.
6. **Preserve independent business reasons and contemporaneous records.** The records should state the non-tax business reasons: complete separation of ownership and management, removal of co-ownership, lender-approved succession changes, and allocation of assets under the agreed 40/60 economics. Retain valuations, meeting approvals, lender consents, and the allocation model.
7. **Test disclosure and reporting early.** The CPA and tax counsel should decide whether Form 8275 or another disclosure is required for any contribution/distribution pairing, and should align the partnership returns, K-1s, capital accounts, and all closing documents with the adopted treatment. Publication 541 describes the potential disclosure requirement for transfers within two years. [IRS Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541).

These safeguards reduce risk; they do not replace a written tax analysis based on the actual closing steps.

### 3.5 Sale of a property after the separation: timing and review

There is no general federal partnership-tax rule requiring a former partner or post-separation owner to wait two years before selling a property received in the separation. A sale may occur after the separation, but it will ordinarily recognize gain or loss based on the property’s tax basis at that time; the distribution does not create a fair-market-value basis step-up. For most distributed property, the recipient’s holding period includes the partnership’s prior holding period. [IRC §735](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A735+edition%3Aprelim%29); [IRS Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541).

The lack of a required waiting period does not eliminate transaction risk. Before any listing agreement, binding letter of intent, purchase contract, deposit, or sale commitment is made, counsel and the CPA should review the particular property for the following:

1. **Prearranged-sale risk.** If a sale was substantially negotiated, required, or effectively committed before the distribution, the IRS could contend that the partnership, rather than the post-separation owner, made the sale or could recast the overall steps according to their substance.
2. **Existing 1031 history.** A prior 1031 exchange does not by itself create a universal two-year holding requirement. However, a related-party 1031 exchange has a specific two-year rule: a disposition within that period can require recognition of deferred gain unless an exception applies. [IRS Form 8824 Instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824).
3. **Prior contributions and special partnership rules.** The seven-year contributed-property and other distribution rules must be tested as of the division/distribution. A later two-year wait does not cure an issue that existed at closing.
4. **Basis and depreciation.** The CPA should calculate the expected gain, depreciation-related character issues, and tax reporting from the carryover/residual basis maintained in the Partition Tool and reconciled tax records.
5. **Loan, title, and contract restrictions.** A sale may still require lender, guarantor, title, insurance, lease, or contract approvals regardless of its federal income-tax treatment.

Accordingly, the working rule is: **do not use a two-year waiting period as a substitute for transaction analysis.** A property may be sold sooner if the CPA and tax counsel approve the actual facts and documents for that property.

## 4. Florida legal, title, and transfer-tax framework

The limited-partnership agreements control many practical questions: approval thresholds, general-partner authority, distribution-in-kind provisions, valuation process, fiduciary standards, and winding-up procedures. Florida law also restricts a limited partnership from making a distribution that violates its agreement or leaves it unable to pay its debts or required preferential rights. [Fla. Stat. §620.1508](https://leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm/ch0713/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&Search_String=&URL=0600-0699%2F0620%2FSections%2F0620.1508.html%2F).

The transaction must also be reviewed separately for Florida documentary stamp tax and title matters. Florida generally imposes documentary stamp tax on deeds and other documents transferring interests in Florida real property. Consideration can include money, exchanged property, and mortgages or other encumbrances, whether or not assumed. Miami-Dade’s rates and surtax rules differ from the statewide rate. [Florida Department of Revenue](https://floridarevenue.com/taxes/taxesfees/Pages/doc_stamp.aspx). A Florida technical assistance advisement reached a no-tax result for a fact pattern involving unencumbered property distributed on dissolution proportionately to partners, but it is fact-specific and not a substitute for a current ruling or counsel’s analysis. [TAA 08B4-004](https://floridarevenue.com/TaxLaw/Documents/TAA%2008B4-004.pdf).

## 5. Transaction structures to compare — including whether BI-1 and BI-2 should continue

Counsel and the CPA should compare at least these implementation paths against the actual governing documents, debt, bases, ownership equalization, and post-closing ownership:

1. **In-kind distribution followed by separate ownership.** The limited partnerships distribute property-holding LLC interests or real estate in a documented allocation, with cash used only for agreed equalization.
2. **Partnership division or other reorganization mechanics.** The existing partnerships divide into successor entities that hold the respective asset pools.
3. **Liquidation of one or both limited partnerships.** The partnerships wind up after distributing assets and resolving liabilities, if permitted and commercially appropriate.
4. **Interest-level equalization before, at, or after asset separation.** The current yellow-column ownership analysis would be equalized to 40/60 during the transaction. Its timing must be analyzed together with the asset allocations and any cash settlement.

### 5.1 Keep BI-1 and BI-2 versus form new entities

The answer is not inherently that retaining BI-1 and BI-2 saves tax, or that replacing them does. The tax result is driven primarily by the actual transfers, debt allocations, owners, and tax classification—not simply by the label on the surviving legal entity.

| Question | Retain BI-1 and/or BI-2 as a continuing entity | Use newly formed post-separation entities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal tax continuity | A qualifying partnership division can allow a resulting partnership to be treated as a continuation, retain the prior partnership’s EIN, and remain subject to certain preexisting elections. The regulation contains detailed continuation and reporting rules. | New entities can simplify post-closing governance, but transferring assets or interests into them must be separately analyzed. Creating a new entity does not erase built-in gain, 1031 history, depreciation history, §704(c) layers, or the disguised-sale analysis. |
| Post-closing ownership | If the post-closing entity has two or more separate tax owners, it may continue to be taxed as a partnership absent a different election. If one person or one tax owner will own the entity, tax counsel must determine the entity’s resulting federal classification and the consequences of ending partnership treatment. | A newly formed LLC may be suitable for a sole owner or family side, but its default federal classification and the tax consequences of funding it must be confirmed before the transfer. |
| Basis and depreciation records | Continuation may be administratively helpful because the entity can retain its historic books, elections, and asset records, subject to the actual transaction rules. It does not create a step-up by itself. | New records can be cleaner operationally, but carryover-basis, depreciation, and 1031 information still must be carried and tracked correctly. A new entity is not a reset of tax basis. |
| Lenders, title, and contracts | Retention may reduce operational disruption where the lender, title, insurance, lease, and vendor arrangements can remain in place. The reported lender willingness to change bad-boy guarantors is a favorable fact, but written consents control. | New entities may provide cleaner separation of management and succession, but may require additional transfers, consents, title work, and Florida documentary-stamp-tax review. |
| Administration | May preserve EINs, bank accounts, tax-return history, and existing operating systems. | May produce a cleaner structure after closing but can add formation, accounting, banking, licensing, contract, and tax-reporting work. |

Before choosing, counsel and the CPA should prepare a side-by-side tax model for at least: (a) BI-1 continuing for the 60% side and BI-2 continuing for the 40% side, if that matches the final economics; (b) a statutory or tax partnership-division structure; and (c) distribution/liquidation followed by new owner entities. The model should compare federal gain, liability-shift consequences, Florida documentary stamp tax, filing/EIN effects, lender/title burden, and long-term depreciation administration. The partnership-division rules describe when a resulting partnership continues and when a new partnership results. [Treas. Reg. §1.708-1(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/section-1.708-1).

No structure should be selected merely because it is described as a “distribution.” The selected form must match the economics, legal authority, debt arrangements, title plan, and tax reporting.

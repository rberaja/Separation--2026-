# Addendum — Tiered Allocation and Settlement Funding

**Companion to:** *Dividing a Jointly-Owned Real Estate Portfolio: A Framework for Equitable Separation Between Unequal Partners*, Version 6.10 (September 2026)  
**Status:** Draft for discussion; not yet incorporated into the authoritative white paper  
**Date:** November 6, 2026

## Purpose and Scope

This addendum supplements the proportional-division and final-settlement framework in the white paper. It does not replace the use of NPV Equity, Debt NPV, Basis True-Up, Bid Difference, or the final settlement calculation. Its purpose is to establish a process for assigning indivisible properties that gives both partners reasonable access to stronger and weaker parts of the portfolio while minimizing the need for a property sale, new borrowing, or personal cash contribution.

A final allocation can be mathematically correct in total and still be inequitable in composition. A cash true-up can correct a dollar difference, but it may not fully correct differences in asset quality, appreciation potential, financing advantage, liquidity, management burden, or strategic importance. The process below therefore considers both the overall 60%/40% result and the mix of properties each partner receives.

## 1. Tiered Allocation of Properties

### 1.1 Establishing the Tiers

Before property selections, rankings, or bids are finalized, the portfolio should be divided into NPV-Equity tiers.

The normal approach is three tiers:

- **Tier 1 — Higher NPV Equity.** The properties with the largest NPV Equity.
- **Tier 2 — Middle NPV Equity.** The properties in the middle range.
- **Tier 3 — Lower NPV Equity.** The remaining properties.

Three tiers are generally sufficient to protect both partners' access to stronger assets without making the process unnecessarily complex. A fourth tier may be used if the portfolio has a clear natural break, such as a small number of unusually large properties and a substantial number of smaller properties. Tier boundaries should follow natural differences in NPV Equity and should not be manipulated merely to favor a preferred result.

Each property, or agreed property group, is assigned to one tier as an indivisible selection unit. The 60% and 40% ownership percentages remain the governing targets within every tier. Accordingly, each partner should have a reasonable opportunity to receive approximately his ownership share of the NPV Equity in the higher, middle, and lower tiers—not merely the correct percentage of the portfolio in total.

Exact proportionality within every tier may not be possible because properties are indivisible. The purpose of the tiers is to avoid systematic concentration of either the strongest or weakest assets in one partner's package. Remaining differences are addressed through later selections and the final settlement.

### 1.2 Required Review of Tier Results

After a tentative allocation, the parties should review both tier-level and portfolio-level results. The review should consider:

- NPV Equity relative to the applicable 60%/40% target;
- remaining tax basis and the resulting Basis True-Up;
- debt characteristics and lender-transfer feasibility;
- NOI and cash-flow characteristics;
- concentration by property type, geography, tenant, or other agreed risk factor;
- liquidity and management burden; and
- each partner's ability to fund any remaining equalizing payment.

The objective is not to make the two packages identical. It is to produce two understandable, proportionate, and financeable packages of whole properties.

## 2. Selection Methods Within Each Tier

The parties should select one method before the process begins. The same method should ordinarily be used in every tier unless both partners agree otherwise.

### 2.1 Alternating Selection List

The partners take turns selecting properties within a tier from an agreed list. The opening position rotates by tier so that neither partner receives the first-pick advantage in every tier. For example, one partner may select first in Tier 1, the other selects first in Tier 2, and the opening position returns to the first partner in Tier 3.

Selections should be monitored against each partner's target NPV Equity for that tier. If a proposed choice would cause a material departure from the applicable 60%/40% target, the parties may defer that property until later in the tier, revise the order, or address the difference through the final settlement.

### 2.2 Confidential Preference Lists and Closed Bids

Each partner prepares a confidential ranked list of desired properties within each tier and submits it to a neutral third party. The neutral party compares the lists and assigns properties where the preferences do not conflict.

If both partners rank the same property at the same or substantially similar level, the neutral party first considers which partner is furthest below that tier's proportional NPV-Equity target after prior assignments. If that measure does not resolve the tie, the partners conduct a one-round closed bid for that property.

Each partner submits a confidential dollar bid to the neutral third party by the agreed deadline. The higher bid receives the property, subject to lender-transfer feasibility, funding capacity, and the requirement that the resulting tier allocation remain within the agreed tolerance of the 60%/40% NPV-Equity targets. Neither partner may revise a submitted bid after seeing the other partner's bid.

The property continues to be credited at its agreed NPV Equity for purposes of proportional allocation. The difference between the winning bid and the agreed NPV Equity is treated as a separate Bid Difference adjustment in the final settlement; it does not replace the property's agreed value or alter the tier target. A winning bid must therefore be supported by available company cash, financing, or another agreed funding source.

If the closed bids are identical, the property is awarded to the partner furthest below the applicable tier target. If the partners are still equally positioned, the neutral third party uses the agreed close-pick procedure or a random draw.

### 2.3 Weighted Preference-Point System

For each tier, each partner receives 100 preference points. Each partner independently allocates those 100 points among the properties in that tier, assigning more points to properties that matter more to that partner. Points have no cash value and cannot be transferred between tiers.

An agreed algorithm, administered by the neutral third party if desired, assigns properties by seeking the allocation that best satisfies all of the following:

- each partner's expressed preference points;
- each partner's 60% or 40% NPV-Equity target within the tier;
- lender-transfer and refinancing feasibility;
- the prohibition on unwanted continuing co-ownership;
- any agreed limits on excessive concentration of risk, property type, or geography; and
- the Cash-Capacity and Settlement-Funding Standard in Section 3.

A property strongly preferred by one partner should ordinarily go to that partner unless doing so would create a material departure from the tier target or an agreed financing constraint. The point system does not replace the final cash settlement; it helps determine who receives the properties before the settlement is calculated.

## 3. Cash-Capacity and Settlement-Funding Standard

### 3.1 Available Settlement Cash

A property allocation should be fair not only in theory, but also in a way that the partners can complete without an unwanted property sale, refinancing, new borrowing, or personal cash contribution. The allocation process should therefore seek to use available company cash to fund the final equalizing settlement to the greatest practical extent.

Before property selections, rankings, or bids are finalized, the parties should determine **Available Settlement Cash**. This is the company's cash and cash equivalents that may be distributed without impairing the continuing operation of the properties or violating any legal, tax, lender, or contractual requirement.

Available Settlement Cash is determined after reserving amounts reasonably needed for:

- current operating expenses and ordinary working capital;
- known repairs, capital expenditures, taxes, insurance, and other obligations;
- debt service, lender-required reserves, and obligations required for loan transfers or releases;
- costs of the separation transaction; and
- any other reserve agreed by both partners.

The resulting amount is the cash available to the common settlement account. It is not additional value created by the process; it is an existing company asset that must be counted once and allocated once.

### 3.2 Allocation Objective

The property-allocation process should seek to minimize the amount of money either partner must obtain outside the company after Available Settlement Cash has been applied.

In practical terms, the preferred allocation is the one that:

1. satisfies the agreed ownership targets as closely as reasonably possible;
2. gives each partner reasonable access to properties across the higher-, middle-, and lower-NPV-Equity tiers;
3. preserves lender feasibility and avoids unwanted continuing co-ownership;
4. uses Available Settlement Cash to reduce the final equalizing payment; and
5. minimizes any remaining amount that would require a property sale, refinancing, borrowing, or personal cash contribution.

A result that is slightly less preferred by one partner may be superior to a more preferred result if the latter creates a large external cash requirement that neither partner wishes or is able to fund.

### 3.3 Use of Cash in the Final Settlement

After all properties have been assigned, the parties calculate the final settlement, including NPV Equity, Debt NPV, Basis True-Up, Bid Difference, and other agreed adjustments. Available Settlement Cash is then placed in the common settlement account and applied to reduce the net amount payable by the partner who received more than his proportional economic share.

Any Available Settlement Cash remaining after the final settlement is funded is distributed according to the partners' ownership percentages. Cash must not be counted both as a separate distribution and as a source of settlement funding.

If the proposed allocation would still require external cash beyond the amount either partner has agreed to contribute, the parties should revise the property assignments, selection order, tier allocations, or bid outcomes before considering a sale, refinancing, or personal contribution.

### 3.4 Effect on Preferences and Bids

Preferences, rankings, and closed bids remain important because they identify which properties matter most to each partner. They do not, however, override the Cash-Capacity and Settlement-Funding Standard.

A property assignment or winning bid should be accepted only if the resulting package can be completed within Available Settlement Cash and the partners' agreed funding limits. If a preferred assignment would require an unwanted sale, new debt, or additional personal cash, the parties should consider the next-best allocation that remains fair, feasible, and financeable.

## 4. Decisions to Be Agreed Before Use

Before applying this addendum, the partners should agree in writing on:

1. the tier boundaries and any property groups treated as one selection unit;
2. the acceptable tolerance around each partner's 60%/40% tier target;
3. the selection method to be used in each tier;
4. the neutral third party, if one will be used;
5. the tie-break and closed-bid procedures, including any funding limit for bids;
6. the calculation of Available Settlement Cash and the required reserves; and
7. the maximum external payment, if any, that either partner is willing to make.

## 5. Relationship to the White Paper

This addendum is a process supplement. The white paper's existing valuation and settlement principles continue to govern: use NPV Equity rather than simple loan-balance equity; calculate any Basis True-Up from the present value of the lost depreciation benefit; treat Bid Difference separately from agreed property value; and use cash in the final settlement without double-counting it.

This addendum is for discussion and planning only. It is not legal, tax, financial, lending, or investment advice. Before implementing an allocation, the partners should obtain appropriate advice from qualified real estate counsel, tax advisors, lenders, and other professionals.
